The roadmap at a glance

Take it, adapt it, and start with one learning objective. Download the editable one-page slide (PowerPoint).

1

Planned

Start from learning outcomes

  • Map AI from program to course, module, and lesson
  • Decide AI’s role for each outcome
  • Protect foundational knowledge
  • Revise outcomes when AI changes them

Ask: Do I know what role AI plays in my learning objectives?

2

Structured

Design how AI enters

  • Choose a stance: formal, optional, or AI as subject
  • Build with purpose: agents, simulations, prompts
  • Add guardrails: boundary statement, custom GPT or Gem
  • Give students who opt out an equivalent path

Ask: Have I designed AI’s place, or am I reacting to it?

3

Supervised

Assess learning, close the loop

  • Collect evidence: transcripts, drafts, reflections
  • Assess the process with the Human-AI Collaboration Rubric
  • Students report their use with LTTR
  • Feed findings back into the plan

Ask: How do I know what students actually learned?

↻ Assess, then revise the plan

In every course: AI literacy · ethical framing · respect for student choice · learning before tools

01

Planned

Start from learning outcomes, not from the tool. Planning cascades from the program to the course, the module, and the lesson. Programs may not be ready yet, but every course can be ready at the level of its own learning objectives.

Protect foundational knowledge. Students can’t judge AI output on a topic they don’t yet know. As I tell my students: if AI gives you a shortcut to a course or module learning outcome, you are harming yourself with the way you use it.

The PCTII model

A code-free design cycle for custom educational AI agents, presented in previous workshops and invited talks. It works for a custom GPT, a Gemini Gem, or a Claude Project.

  1. PrepareDefine the goal, the outcomes, and why the agent is needed
  2. CreateWrite instructions, conversation starters, and boundaries
  3. TestTest from several roles, including a student trying to misuse it
  4. ImproveRefine based on what testing revealed
  5. IntegrateDeploy with a policy, boundaries, and follow-up

A prompt library for ethical, learning-centered AI use

Many students want to use AI but aren’t sure how to do it without undermining their own learning. In an online people-management course, I offered students an optional, ungraded library of course-specific prompts. The goal was to give them a structured way to use AI that keeps them doing the thinking. Students chose whether to use it, and those who didn’t lost nothing. Students who declined, whether they preferred to learn independently or objected to AI on principle, were respected by the design. A paper describing the library and what students reported is forthcoming.

Writing course-specific prompts for your students

A good course prompt keeps the student doing the thinking. Before you share one, check that it:

  • Ties to a specific course or module learning objective
  • Asks the student to bring something of their own: a draft, an answer, an example
  • Asks AI to question, check, or critique, not to produce the graded work
  • Comes with a boundary statement: what the prompt is for, and what it is not for

Two generic starters to adapt to your course:

“I’m studying [concept] in [course]. Before you explain anything, ask me three questions to check what I already understand. Then tell me where my answers were incomplete.”

“Here is my explanation of [concept] for someone new to the field: [your draft]. Point out what is unclear or inaccurate, but don’t rewrite it for me.”

04

Policy and student choice

A course AI policy can rest on four words, the LTTR framework. Students who opt out of AI get an equivalent path to every learning outcome.

Literate

Know the tools, the rules, and how to judge the output.

Transparent

Disclose when and how AI was used.

Traceable

Keep the drafts, prompts, and reflections that show the thinking.

Responsible

Ask not only whether you can use AI, but whether you should.
